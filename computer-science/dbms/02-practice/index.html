Section C — Assertion & Reason

Instructions for this section: Each question contains an Assertion (A) and a Reason (R). Choose the option that correctly describes the truth of both statements and their relationship.

A — Both Assertion and Reason are true, and Reason is the correct explanation of Assertion.
B — Both Assertion and Reason are true, but Reason does not correctly explain Assertion.
C — Assertion is true, Reason is false.

9 ASSERTION – REASON

Assertion (A): BCNF (Boyce-Codd Normal Form) represents a more rigorous standard of normalisation than 3NF because it is designed to eliminate redundancy and update anomalies that 3NF is not always capable of eliminating on its own.

Reason (R): There exist schema structures in which all 3NF conditions are technically satisfied, yet certain functional dependency relationships still permit update anomalies — situations where a valid update in one place creates inconsistency elsewhere — and BCNF addresses precisely these cases by imposing a stricter constraint.

10 ASSERTION – REASON

Assertion (A): The transition from physical bookkeeping to spreadsheets and the transition from spreadsheets to a relational DBMS are fundamentally the same kind of improvement — each makes the preceding system faster, more reliable, and better at the same tasks, in a continuous progression along a single axis of improvement.

Reason (R): The physical-to-electronic transition is a qualitative change — it changes the nature of what is possible by making durability a software concern and retrieval algorithmic rather than manual; the spreadsheet-to-DBMS transition is a different kind of advance — it changes the data management contract by adding consistency, atomicity, concurrency guarantees, and constraint enforcement that the flat-file model is structurally incapable of providing. These are different axes of improvement, not the same axis repeated.

After This Practice

If any question surprised you, compare the options against the scenario itself and the core ideas behind it. The questions above target the most persistent misconceptions in data management history — particularly the hard-ceiling nature of scalability failure, what machine-readable data actually enables, why solving durability does not solve consistency, and the precise difference between the two kinds of advances that the three main eras represent.

  • Scalability failure is architectural, not a staffing problem — no number of clerks can make physical systems coordinate real-time global data.
  • Machine-readable encoding removes the human from the computation loop — this is a conceptual leap, not a formatting choice.
  • Durability and consistency are separate axes — solving one does not touch the other. A spreadsheet can be perfectly backed up and still be inconsistent.
  • The two major transitions are different kinds of improvements: physical-to-electronic changes what is possible; spreadsheet-to-DBMS changes the engineering contract.
  • NoSQL extended the ecosystem — it did not replace the relational architecture. These coexist for different problem classes.
  • BCNF exists because 3NF is not always sufficient — understanding why requires understanding what kinds of anomalies 3NF can still permit.
  • The six DBMS parameters are permanent requirements, not era-specific features. Every system is evaluated against all six, simultaneously.
